```html
<p>A startup needs a website because it is the only place online you control. Social profiles are borrowed ground — the platform decides who sees you, changes the rules without asking, and can remove the account entirely. A website is an asset you own, and it is the one channel where nobody stands between you and the person you are trying to reach.</p>

<p>Almost no founder needs convincing of that. The useful questions are what the first version has to contain, what can wait, and whether to build it yourself. Those are what this covers.</p>

<h2>What a website does that a social profile cannot</h2>

<h3>You own it</h3>

<p>Every follower you have is a relationship the platform mediates. Reach is throttled, terms change, and accounts are occasionally suspended by automated systems with no meaningful appeal. Founders who built their entire presence on one platform and lost the account do not get it back.</p>

<p>Your website, your domain and your email list are yours. That is the whole argument, and it is sufficient on its own.</p>

<h3>It is where people arrive from search</h3>

<p>People searching for what you do will find a website. Social profiles rank occasionally; they are not built to answer specific questions and they do not compete for informational queries.</p>

<p>This compounds. A site that answers the questions your customers ask keeps producing enquiries for years, at no additional cost, long after a social post has disappeared from every feed.</p>

<h3>It is checked before people commit</h3>

<p>Whatever the first contact is — a referral, an ad, a conversation at an event — people look you up before deciding. A professional site confirms you are real. No site, or an obviously abandoned one, raises the question of whether you will still exist next year.</p>

<p>For a startup this matters disproportionately, because you have no track record doing the reassuring for you.</p>

<h3>It is where you can explain yourself properly</h3>

<p>Social formats are short and interruptive. A website is where the full argument lives — what you do, who it is for, how it works, what it costs, why you rather than the alternative. Buyers who need that detail cannot get it from a profile.</p>

<h2>What the first version actually needs</h2>

<p>Less than most founders assume. A small, clear, fast site beats an ambitious unfinished one, and it can ship this month.</p>

<h3>The five pages that matter</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Home</strong> — what you do, who for, and what to do next. Answered above the fold, in plain language.</li>
  <li><strong>What you offer</strong> — one page per distinct thing, or one page if there is only one. Concrete, not abstract.</li>
  <li><strong>About</strong> — who is behind this. For a startup this page does more work than it does for an established company, because people are assessing the team.</li>
  <li><strong>Contact</strong> — an obvious way to reach you that someone actually monitors.</li>
  <li><strong>Legal</strong> — privacy policy and anything your jurisdiction requires.</li>
</ul>

<h3>What has to be true of it</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>It says what you do in the first sentence.</strong> Not a mission statement. A description a stranger would understand.</li>
  <li><strong>It works properly on a phone.</strong> Most first visits are mobile — the specifics are in <a href="/blog/does-your-website-need-a-mobile-makeover">works properly on a phone</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>It loads quickly.</strong> Nobody waits for a company they have not heard of.</li>
  <li><strong>Contact details are easy to find.</strong> Hidden contact information reads as evasive.</li>
  <li><strong>It has a real domain and real email.</strong> A free-provider email address on business cards undercuts everything else.</li>
  <li><strong>The main action is obvious.</strong> One thing you want visitors to do, clearly presented — the reasoning is in <a href="/blog/ways-to-make-your-website-s-landing-page-a-conversion-machine">landing page principles</a>.</li>
</ul>

<h3>The credibility details founders skip</h3>

<p>Small things carry disproportionate weight when nobody has heard of you: real photographs of real people rather than stock; a physical location or at least a city; a named human to contact rather than a form into a void; and specifics — actual clients, actual numbers, actual examples — rather than adjectives.</p>

<p>One genuine, named piece of evidence outperforms a page of confident claims.</p>

<h2>What to skip in version one</h2>

<p>Founders routinely delay launching for features nobody has asked for.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>A blog with no plan to maintain it.</strong> Three posts and then silence is worse than no blog, because it dates visibly.</li>
  <li><strong>Custom illustration and animation.</strong> Expensive, slow, and rarely the reason someone buys.</li>
  <li><strong>Live chat nobody staffs.</strong> An unanswered chat widget is worse than none.</li>
  <li><strong>A newsletter with nothing to send.</strong> Add the signup when you have something to say.</li>
  <li><strong>Ecommerce you do not yet need.</strong> If you sell three things occasionally, an invoice link is enough.</li>
  <li><strong>Accounts and logins</strong> before there is anything to log into.</li>
  <li><strong>Elaborate analytics.</strong> One conversion goal, tracked correctly, is worth more than a dashboard nobody reads.</li>
</ul>

<p>The pattern: build what today's visitor needs. Everything else is a maintenance obligation with no current return.</p>

<h2>Build it yourself, or pay someone?</h2>

<p>Honest guidance, since the answer is often "yourself, for now".</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Approach</th><th>Suits</th><th>Watch out for</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><strong>Hosted builder, yourself</strong></td><td>Pre-revenue, five pages, needs to exist this week</td><td>Portability later; keep your own domain</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Freelancer</strong></td><td>Some budget, want it to look considered, scope is contained</td><td>Availability when you need changes</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Agency</strong></td><td>Funded, the site is a primary channel, several disciplines needed</td><td>Cost relative to stage</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>A reasonable sequence for most startups: build a simple version yourself to establish presence, learn from real visitors what people actually ask, then invest properly once you know what the site needs to do. Commissioning an expensive site before you understand your customers means paying to encode assumptions.</p>

<p>If you do hire, knowing <a href="/blog/what-is-the-difference-between-web-designing-and-web-development">who you actually need to hire</a> prevents paying for the wrong skill.</p>

<h3>Own the accounts, whatever you choose</h3>

<p>Register the domain yourself, in the company's name, with your own payment method. Keep the registrar login. The same for hosting, analytics and any account created for you.</p>

<p>This sounds obvious and is regularly ignored, and it is one of the more painful problems to discover later — a founder unable to move their site because a former contractor holds the domain has very few good options.</p>

<h2>Writing the copy, which is the hard part</h2>

<p>Founders usually find the writing harder than the building, and it matters more. A plain site with clear copy outperforms a beautiful site that leaves visitors unsure what you sell.</p>

<h3>Lead with the problem you solve</h3>

<p>Visitors do not arrive interested in your company. They arrive with a problem. Copy that opens by naming that problem — in their words, not your internal terminology — earns the next thirty seconds. Copy that opens with your founding story does not.</p>

<h3>Be concrete about who it is for</h3>

<p>Naming your audience explicitly feels like turning people away and does the opposite. "For operations teams at manufacturers with 50 to 500 staff" tells the right person they are in the right place. "For businesses of all sizes" tells nobody anything.</p>

<h3>Say the price, or say something about it</h3>

<p>Startups routinely hide pricing entirely, which loses the people who would have bought and wastes time on those who never could. If exact pricing is genuinely impossible, give a range, a starting point, or a description of how it is calculated. Silence reads as expensive.</p>

<h3>Use the words your customers use</h3>

<p>The vocabulary inside a company drifts from the vocabulary of its market quickly. Take the phrasing from actual customer emails, sales calls and support tickets. If customers say "bookkeeping" and your site says "financial operations infrastructure", you have made yourself harder to find and harder to understand.</p>

<h3>Cut it, then cut it again</h3>

<p>First drafts of startup copy are almost always too long, because everything feels essential when you are close to it. Remove anything a visitor would not need on a first visit. What remains is usually clearer and does the job better.</p>

<h2>The online presence beyond the site</h2>

<p>The website is the centre, not the whole of it. Three things are worth doing early because they are cheap and compound.</p>

<p><strong>Claim your business listings.</strong> A verified local listing is often the first result for your name and is free. It also handles the "are they real" question for anyone checking.</p>

<p><strong>Secure your handles.</strong> Register the name on relevant platforms even if you will not post yet — reclaiming a taken handle later is usually impossible.</p>

<p><strong>Make sure your details match everywhere.</strong> Name, address and phone consistent across your site and every listing. Inconsistencies undermine trust and complicate local search.</p>

<p>Beyond that, be where your customers are rather than everywhere. One channel maintained properly beats five abandoned ones, and abandoned profiles are visible evidence of a company that started things and stopped.</p>

<h2>Getting found once the site exists</h2>

<p>A new site has no search visibility on day one. That is normal, and there are a few things worth doing immediately rather than waiting.</p>

<p><strong>Verify the site in <a href="https://search.google.com/search-console/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Google Search Console</a>.</strong> Free, takes minutes, and it is the only place you can see which queries you appear for and whether anything is blocking indexing. Submit a sitemap while you are there.</p>

<p><strong>Check the site is actually indexable.</strong> A surprising number of new sites launch with search engines still blocked from the staging configuration. Search for your domain; if nothing appears after a couple of weeks, that is the first thing to check.</p>

<p><strong>Write a real title and description for each page.</strong> These are what appear in search results. Default titles like "Home" waste the one line you get to persuade someone to click.</p>

<p><strong>Expect it to take time.</strong> A new domain has no history, so ranking for anything competitive takes months. In the meantime, your site does its most valuable work for people who already know your name and are checking you out — which is a real job, not a consolation prize.</p>

<p>Do not buy links, submit to hundreds of directories, or hire anyone promising fast rankings for a new domain. Those offers exist because new founders are the easiest people to sell them to, and the tactics range from ineffective to actively harmful.</p>

<h2>Mistakes that cost startups</h2>

<p><strong>Waiting for perfect.</strong> A simple site live today produces more than an ambitious one launching next quarter.</p>

<p><strong>Describing the vision instead of the offer.</strong> Visitors want to know what you do now, not what you intend to become.</p>

<p><strong>Jargon and abstraction.</strong> "Empowering seamless transformation" describes nothing. Say what you do in the words your customers use.</p>

<p><strong>No contact route.</strong> A form with no response commitment, and no phone or email, loses people who want to talk to someone.</p>

<p><strong>Not owning the domain.</strong> The single most avoidable problem in this article.</p>

<p><strong>Launching and abandoning.</strong> A site with a two-year-old copyright notice and a dead blog is a live signal of neglect.</p>

<p><strong>Copying a competitor's site.</strong> You inherit their positioning and their compromises, and you look like the derivative option.</p>

<h2>When to invest properly</h2>

<p>Signals that the simple version has done its job and it is time to spend more:</p>

<ul>
  <li>The site is producing enquiries and better structure would produce more</li>
  <li>You are spending on ads and the destination is limiting the return</li>
  <li>You have learned what customers actually ask, and the current site does not answer it</li>
  <li>The builder you started on is now a constraint</li>
  <li>You have proof — clients, results, testimonials — that the current site does not present well</li>
  <li>Search is producing traffic and better content would produce more</li>
</ul>

<p>The common thread is evidence. Investing because the site feels basic is guessing; investing because you know what it should do differently is not.</p>

<h2>Keeping it alive without a marketing team</h2>

<p>The most common failure after launch is not a bad site — it is a site nobody touches again. A modest maintenance habit prevents almost all of the damage.</p>

<p><strong>A quarterly review, taking an hour.</strong> Check that the copy still describes what you actually sell, that contact details are current, that nothing is broken, and that the footer year is not two years old. Businesses change faster than their websites, and the gap becomes visible surprisingly quickly.</p>

<p><strong>Add evidence as you earn it.</strong> Each new client, result or testimonial is something the site can use. Adding one genuine piece of proof a quarter builds a credible site over a year without a content programme.</p>

<p><strong>Keep the software updated.</strong> Whatever you built on needs security updates applied. This is ten minutes a month and it is the difference between a working site and a compromised one.</p>

<p><strong>Answer the questions you keep receiving.</strong> If three prospects ask the same thing, the site should answer it. This is the cheapest and most reliable source of content you will ever have, and it improves conversion as well as search visibility.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>Why do startups need a website?</h3>
<p>Because it is the only online presence you own. Social platforms control your reach, change their rules, and can remove your account. A website is also where search traffic arrives, where people check you before committing, and where you can explain your offer at the length it needs.</p>

<h3>Can a startup just use social media instead?</h3>
<p>Not safely. Social profiles are borrowed ground — reach is throttled, terms change, and accounts can be lost. They also do not answer the specific questions people search for, and they cannot hold the detail a considered purchase requires. Use social to reach people; use your site to convert and to own the relationship.</p>

<h3>What should a startup website include?</h3>
<p>Home, what you offer, about, contact and the legal pages. It must say what you do in the first sentence, work well on a phone, load quickly, make contact easy, use a real domain and email, and present one obvious next action. Everything beyond that can wait.</p>

<h3>Should I build the website myself or hire someone?</h3>
<p>Build a simple version yourself if you are pre-revenue and need presence quickly. Hire once you know what the site needs to do — after real visitors have shown you what they ask. Commissioning an expensive site before that means paying to encode assumptions you have not tested.</p>

<h3>How much should a startup spend on a website?</h3>
<p>Enough to be credible and clear, and no more until the site has proven what it needs to be. The staged approach — simple first, invest once you have evidence — usually costs less overall than building elaborately upfront and rebuilding once you understand your customers.</p>

<h2>What to do this week</h2>

<p>Register the domain in your own name. Write one sentence describing what you do, in words a customer would use, and check whether a stranger understands it. Then publish five pages.</p>

<p>That is a working website, and it will teach you more about what your site needs than any amount of planning. Watch what people ask you after they have seen it — the questions they still have are exactly what version two should answer, and no amount of upfront strategy would have identified them as reliably.</p>
```
