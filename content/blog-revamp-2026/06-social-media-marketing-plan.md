```html
<p>A social media marketing plan is a written decision about four things: what you are trying to achieve, who you are trying to reach, which channels you will use to reach them, and how you will know whether it worked. Everything else — the calendar, the tools, the tone of voice — follows from those four answers.</p>

<p>Most social media activity fails not because the posts are bad but because none of those four questions were answered before posting started. What follows is the order to answer them in.</p>

<h2>Plan, strategy, or calendar?</h2>

<p>These three words get used interchangeably and it causes real confusion in briefs, so it is worth separating them.</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Term</th><th>What it actually is</th><th>Time horizon</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><strong>Strategy</strong></td><td>The choice of who you are for and why they should care — what you will and will not be known for</td><td>12–24 months</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Plan</strong></td><td>How that strategy gets executed: channels, pillars, cadence, resourcing, budget, measurement</td><td>3–12 months</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Calendar</strong></td><td>What gets published, when, by whom</td><td>2–8 weeks</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>A team that has a calendar but no plan is busy. A team with a plan but no strategy is consistent about nothing in particular. Build them in that order — strategy, plan, calendar — and each one constrains the next usefully.</p>

<h2>Step 1: Tie objectives to business outcomes</h2>

<p>Start by writing down what social media is supposed to change about your business. Not "grow our following" — that is an activity metric, and a growing following that never buys anything is a cost centre with good optics.</p>

<p>Useful objectives look like: shorten the sales cycle by making prospects aware of us before they enquire; reduce support load by answering common questions publicly; recruit better candidates by showing what working here is like; defend branded search by owning what appears when someone looks us up.</p>

<p>Each of those implies different content, different channels, and different definitions of success. Pick one primary objective and at most one secondary. Plans that list five objectives generally deliver none, because the content required for each pulls in a different direction.</p>

<h3>Write the objective as a sentence you could be wrong about</h3>

<p>"Increase brand awareness" cannot be wrong, which makes it useless. "Within six months, prospects who enquire will mention having seen our work before contacting us" is a claim that can fail, which makes it a real objective. You will know within six months whether it happened.</p>

<h2>Step 2: Define the audience narrowly</h2>

<p>The instinct is to describe the widest plausible audience, because a bigger number feels safer. It produces content addressed to nobody.</p>

<p>Get specific about three things: what this person is actually trying to do, what they currently believe about the problem you solve, and where they already spend attention. That third point is the one that decides your channels, and it should be based on observation rather than assumption — ask your last twenty customers where they saw you, and the answer is frequently not where your team assumed.</p>

<p>Segment by situation rather than demographics where you can. "Operations manager at a 50–200 person manufacturer who has just been made responsible for the website" is actionable. "Males 25–45" is not.</p>

<h2>Step 3: Audit what already exists</h2>

<p>Before deciding what to do, establish what is already happening. Most organisations have more social presence than they think, and some of it is working against them.</p>

<ul>
  <li>Every account in the company's name, including ones nobody has logged into for years</li>
  <li>Who controls each one — this is where the real risk sits, because access frequently rests with someone who left</li>
  <li>Which posts over the past year got genuine engagement, and what they had in common</li>
  <li>What competitors publish, and more usefully, what they consistently ignore</li>
  <li>What people already say about you unprompted, which is your actual brand position rather than your intended one</li>
</ul>

<p>Dormant accounts should be closed or claimed and clearly marked, not left drifting. An abandoned profile with a 2018 cover image is a live trust signal to anyone who finds it.</p>

<h2>Step 4: Choose channels deliberately</h2>

<p>Being on every platform is the most common and most expensive mistake. Each channel is a standing commitment to produce a format, maintain a cadence and answer replies. Two channels done properly beat five done thinly, every time.</p>

<p>Choose against three tests, in this order:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Is the audience actually there and receptive?</strong> Present is not enough — people are on many platforms in a mode where your content would be an intrusion.</li>
  <li><strong>Can you sustain the native format?</strong> A platform built on short-form video needs video capability every week, not a burst in month one.</li>
  <li><strong>Does it serve the objective?</strong> Channels good for recruitment are often not the ones good for demand generation.</li>
</ol>

<p>Write down the channels you are deliberately <em>not</em> using and why. That single line prevents most of the "should we be on this?" conversations that derail plans six weeks in.</p>

<h2>Step 5: Set content pillars</h2>

<p>Content pillars are the three to five recurring themes you will be known for. They are what stop a calendar becoming a scramble for something to post on Thursday.</p>

<p>A workable set covers different jobs rather than different topics. For a professional services firm that might be: demonstrated expertise (work, methods, results); education (answering what clients actually ask); perspective (a defensible view on something contested in your field); and people (who does the work).</p>

<p>Test each candidate pillar against three questions: can we produce this every week without straining, would our audience genuinely care, and could a competitor publish the identical post? If the answer to the last one is yes, it is not a pillar — it is filler.</p>

<h3>Pillars are how brand building actually happens</h3>

<p>Building a brand on social media is not a separate activity you bolt on. It is the compound effect of being recognisably consistent about the same few things for long enough that people can predict what you stand for. That is precisely what pillars enforce. Brands that feel strong on social are almost always brands that narrowed rather than broadened.</p>

<p>If your audit in step 3 suggested people do not recognise you consistently — or recognise you for something you no longer do — the problem may sit upstream of social entirely, and it is worth assessing <a href="/blog/when-to-rebrand-your-company">whether the brand itself needs work</a> before spending a year being consistent about the wrong thing.</p>

<h2>Step 6: Decide cadence honestly</h2>

<p>Set the cadence you can maintain in your worst month, not your best. Consistency compounds; bursts followed by silence do not, and a gap reads as abandonment.</p>

<p>Build the calendar around a mix of planned and reactive content, roughly 70/30. The planned portion comes from your pillars and known dates. The reactive portion is deliberately left empty so that when something genuinely relevant happens, there is room for it. Calendars filled to 100% two months ahead cannot respond to anything and get abandoned at the first disruption.</p>

<h2>Step 7: Assign ownership before launch</h2>

<p>Plans die in the gap between "we agreed to do this" and "someone specific does it on Tuesday." Name a person for each of: creating content, approving it, publishing it, responding to comments and messages, and escalating anything sensitive.</p>

<p>Response ownership is the one most often left unassigned, and it is the one that causes damage. An unanswered complaint sitting publicly under a post for four days does more harm than the four posts above it did good. Set a response-time expectation and make it someone's actual job.</p>

<p>Write down the escalation path too — who decides when something is a problem, and who speaks for the company when it is. Deciding that during an incident is how organisations make things worse.</p>

<h2>Step 8: Define measurement before you start</h2>

<p>Decide what you will measure while you still have no results, because deciding afterwards guarantees you pick whichever number looks best.</p>

<p>Separate three tiers and do not mix them:</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Tier</th><th>Examples</th><th>What it tells you</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Activity</td><td>Posts published, response time</td><td>Whether the plan is being executed. Internal only.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Engagement</td><td>Saves, shares, comments, profile visits</td><td>Whether the content resonates. A leading indicator, not an outcome.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Outcome</td><td>Enquiries, branded search volume, assisted conversions, applications</td><td>Whether it affected the business. This is what the plan is judged on.</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>Favour saves and shares over likes. A like costs nothing; a share means someone attached their own reputation to your content. Attribution on social is genuinely difficult and worth treating carefully rather than confidently — the mechanics of that, including what these numbers can and cannot prove, are covered in our guide to <a href="/blog/tips-for-improving-social-media-roi">measuring social media ROI properly</a>.</p>

<h3>Tag your links before the first post, not after</h3>

<p>Every link you publish should carry campaign parameters so that traffic arriving from social is identifiable in your analytics rather than dissolving into direct or referral traffic. <a href="https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10917952" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Google Analytics documents the UTM parameters</a> it reads; agree a naming convention for source, medium and campaign before anyone publishes, because retrofitting consistency across six months of inconsistent tags is not practical.</p>

<p>Expect a meaningful share of genuine social-driven visits to still show as direct. People see something on a phone, and come back later on a laptop by typing your name. That gap is one of the reasons branded search volume is a more honest indicator of social working than click counts are.</p>

<h2>Adapting one idea across channels</h2>

<p>Earlier I said reposting an identical asset everywhere does not work. The alternative is not producing separate content for each channel, which no small team can sustain. It is producing one substantial thing and adapting it deliberately.</p>

<p>The method: make one piece of real substance — a project breakdown, a considered answer to a question clients keep asking, a genuine point of view. That is the source. Then derive from it, respecting each channel's native format rather than translating the same post into different aspect ratios.</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Channel type</th><th>What the source becomes</th><th>What fails there</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Professional network</td><td>The argument, written out, with the reasoning visible</td><td>Link-only posts with no substance in the post itself</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Short-form video</td><td>One specific claim from it, demonstrated visually in under a minute</td><td>Talking-head summaries of the whole piece</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Image-led feed</td><td>The single most striking visual artefact — a before/after, a diagram</td><td>Text rendered as an image because it was easier</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Owned channels</td><td>The full piece, where nobody throttles your reach</td><td>Treating it as an afterthought once social is done</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>One source piece a fortnight, adapted properly, outperforms daily posts assembled from nothing. It also solves the resourcing problem in step 7, because you are commissioning one substantial thing rather than fourteen small ones.</p>

<h3>Write the voice down</h3>

<p>If more than one person publishes, document the voice in a page or less: three adjectives it is, three it is not, two example posts that got it right, one that did not, and the specific words you avoid. Without this, consistency depends on whoever happens to be posting, and the brand-building effect described in step 5 never accumulates because there is nothing stable to recognise.</p>

<h2>Where paid fits</h2>

<p>Organic reach on most major platforms is limited and does not scale on demand. A plan that assumes organic distribution alone will reach a large cold audience is planning on something the platforms no longer reliably provide.</p>

<p>The pragmatic split: organic builds credibility with people who already know you and proves which messages work; paid takes the messages that already proved themselves and puts them in front of people who do not know you yet. Using paid to distribute untested creative is how budgets disappear quietly. The mechanics of running the paid side are a separate discipline, covered in <a href="/blog/social-media-advertising-tips-and-tricks">paid social advertising</a>.</p>

<h2>A one-page plan template</h2>

<p>If the plan does not fit on one page, it will not be read. Fill in these ten lines:</p>

<ol>
  <li>Primary objective, written as a claim that could turn out false</li>
  <li>Secondary objective (optional — leave blank if unsure)</li>
  <li>Audience, described by situation</li>
  <li>Channels we will use</li>
  <li>Channels we are deliberately skipping, and why</li>
  <li>Content pillars (three to five)</li>
  <li>Cadence per channel, set at worst-month capacity</li>
  <li>Named owners: create, approve, publish, respond, escalate</li>
  <li>Outcome metrics and review date</li>
  <li>What would make us stop or change course</li>
</ol>

<p>That last line is the one teams skip and the one that saves the most money. Agreeing in advance what failure looks like is what allows you to stop a channel that is not working without it becoming a political argument.</p>

<h2>Mistakes that recur</h2>

<p><strong>Starting with the calendar.</strong> Deciding what to post before deciding who it is for produces content that is technically consistent and strategically empty.</p>

<p><strong>Copying a competitor's channel mix.</strong> You are copying the output of their constraints and objectives, which are not yours.</p>

<p><strong>Treating every platform as a broadcast surface.</strong> Reposting one asset everywhere ignores that each platform has its own native format and expectations, and audiences notice.</p>

<p><strong>Judging it in six weeks.</strong> Organic social compounds slowly. Six weeks measures whether you executed, not whether it worked. Review at a quarter, decide at two.</p>

<p><strong>Chasing follower count.</strong> Followers are a vanity number that correlates weakly with outcomes. A small audience of the right people outperforms a large audience of the wrong ones, and costs less to serve.</p>

<h2>When this approach is not appropriate</h2>

<p>A structured organic plan is the wrong first investment in three situations, and it is worth being honest about them.</p>

<p>If you need demand in the next quarter, organic social will not deliver it. Paid channels and search will. Organic is a compounding asset with a slow start.</p>

<p>If nobody internally can produce content weekly and there is no budget to outsource it, do not start. A dormant account is worse than no account, because it is visible evidence of abandonment.</p>

<p>If your product or service is genuinely undifferentiated, social will make that clearer rather than fix it. Consistency amplifies whatever is already there. That is a positioning problem, and it needs solving first.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>What is the difference between a social media strategy and a social media marketing plan?</h3>
<p>The strategy is the decision about who you are for and what you will be known for. The plan is how that gets executed — channels, content pillars, cadence, ownership and measurement. Strategy is the direction; the plan is the operating detail. Write the strategy first, because it constrains every choice in the plan.</p>

<h3>How many social media platforms should a business be on?</h3>
<p>Usually one or two done properly. Each channel is an ongoing commitment to a format, a cadence and a response obligation. The correct number is the number you can sustain in a bad month while still meeting your response-time commitment.</p>

<h3>How long before a social media plan shows results?</h3>
<p>Engagement signals move within weeks. Outcome metrics such as enquiries and branded search typically take two to three quarters, because organic social works by accumulating familiarity rather than by capturing existing demand. Review quarterly; judge at two quarters.</p>

<h3>How do you build a brand on social media?</h3>
<p>By being recognisably consistent about a small number of things for long enough that people can predict what you stand for. In practice that means narrow content pillars, a stable voice, and the discipline to keep publishing within them when it feels repetitive — well past the point where it stops feeling novel internally.</p>

<h3>Should the plan include paid social?</h3>
<p>Almost always, but as a second stage. Use organic to establish which messages genuinely resonate, then put budget behind the ones that proved themselves. Paying to distribute untested creative is the most common way social budgets are wasted.</p>

<h2>Start here</h2>

<p>If you write nothing else this week, write the primary objective as a sentence that could turn out to be false, and the list of channels you are deliberately not using. Those two lines eliminate most of the decisions that stall social media plans, and they take about twenty minutes.</p>
```
