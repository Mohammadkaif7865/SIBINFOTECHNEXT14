```html
<p>A company should rebrand when its identity actively misrepresents what the business now is, or actively prevents it becoming what it intends to be. Not when the logo feels tired internally, and not when a new executive wants to make a mark — both common, and neither a reason.</p>

<p>The test is whether the current brand is costing you something specific that you can name. If you cannot name it, a rebrand will be expensive and will not fix anything.</p>

<h2>Three different things get called rebranding</h2>

<p>Much of the confusion in these decisions comes from treating one word as covering three very different projects.</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th></th><th>What changes</th><th>Risk</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><strong>Refresh</strong></td><td>Visual identity modernised — typography, palette, logo refined. Name, positioning and promise unchanged.</td><td>Low. Recognition is preserved.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Repositioning</strong></td><td>What you stand for and who you serve. Visual identity may barely change.</td><td>Moderate. Requires sustained communication.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Full rebrand</strong></td><td>Name, identity and positioning together, often the domain as well.</td><td>High. Recognition resets to near zero.</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>Most companies that believe they need a full rebrand need a refresh, and a meaningful number that undertake a refresh actually needed repositioning — they changed how they look while the underlying problem was what they were saying.</p>

<p>Establishing which of the three you need is the first decision, and it changes the budget by an order of magnitude.</p>

<h2>Reasons that genuinely justify it</h2>

<h3>The business has changed and the brand has not</h3>

<p>You started as one thing and are now another. The name references a service you have discontinued, the identity signals a market you have left, or the positioning describes a company you no longer are.</p>

<p>This is the clearest case, and the signal is concrete: prospects arrive with the wrong expectation, and your team spends the first ten minutes of every conversation correcting it.</p>

<h3>The name is an active constraint</h3>

<p>Geographic names that limit expansion. Product names that limit range. Names that are unpronounceable in a market you are entering, or mean something unfortunate in another language. Names too similar to a competitor's, causing genuine confusion.</p>

<p>These are structural limits, not aesthetic preferences, and they do not resolve on their own.</p>

<h3>A merger or acquisition</h3>

<p>Two brands, one company. The decision is which identity survives, whether to combine, or whether to create something new. What matters is deciding deliberately rather than allowing an ambiguous transitional state to persist for two years, which is the common failure.</p>

<h3>Reputational damage that has attached to the name</h3>

<p>Sometimes a name carries an association that will not fade. This is legitimate — but only alongside genuine change to whatever caused it. Renaming without fixing the underlying issue is read exactly as what it is, and usually attracts more attention than the original problem.</p>

<h3>The audience has moved</h3>

<p>Your buyers are now a different generation, a different sector, or a different seniority, and the brand still speaks to the previous one. This is often better solved by repositioning than by a full rebrand.</p>

<h3>Legal necessity</h3>

<p>A trademark conflict or a licensing arrangement ending. Rare, and it removes the decision entirely.</p>

<h2>Reasons that do not hold up</h2>

<p><strong>"It feels dated to us."</strong> Your team sees it daily; customers see it occasionally. Internal fatigue arrives years before customer fatigue, and it is not evidence of anything.</p>

<p><strong>A new leader wants to make a mark.</strong> Common, expensive, and rarely stated aloud as the actual reason. Worth naming honestly in the room, because it changes the discussion.</p>

<p><strong>A competitor rebranded.</strong> You have no idea whether theirs worked, and following them makes you the derivative one.</p>

<p><strong>Sales are down.</strong> Establish why first. If the cause is the product, the price, the market or the sales process, a rebrand spends significant money without touching it — and consumes the attention that should have gone to diagnosis.</p>

<p><strong>The website looks old.</strong> That is a website problem with a website solution. A redesign is a fraction of the cost and does not reset recognition.</p>

<h2>What a rebrand costs beyond the invoice</h2>

<p>This is the side that gets underweighted, because it is harder to quantify than a design fee.</p>

<h3>Recognition resets</h3>

<p>Every mention of you online, every business card, every bookmark, every person who half-remembers your name — a full rebrand starts that accumulation again. For an established business this is often the largest cost, and it does not appear on any quote.</p>

<h3>Search visibility, if the domain changes</h3>

<p>A domain change is a full site migration. Every URL moves, and if that is not mapped and redirected carefully, you lose the search visibility built over years.</p>

<p>It is survivable and routinely survived — but it requires the migration to be treated as a technical project with its own plan, not as an afterthought to the design work. The mechanics are the same as any replatform, covered in <a href="/blog/things-to-consider-before-redesigning-your-website">the migration mechanics</a>. Google publishes <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes" target="_blank" rel="noopener">specific guidance for site moves involving URL changes</a>, and following it closely is what separates a rebrand that keeps its traffic from one that does not.</p>

<p>If the name changes but the domain need not, keeping it is worth serious consideration. Plenty of companies operate successfully under a name that does not exactly match their domain.</p>

<h3>Internal disruption</h3>

<p>Templates, signatures, contracts, signage, uniforms, packaging, presentations, invoices, the CRM, the app store listing. A rebrand touches more systems than anyone lists at the outset, and the long tail runs for months after launch.</p>

<h3>Customer confusion during transition</h3>

<p>There is a period where existing customers are unsure whether they are dealing with the same company. Communicated well it is brief; communicated poorly it produces support load and churn.</p>

<h2>What a rebrand actually involves</h2>

<p>Scoping this properly early is what prevents a project that was quoted as a logo becoming a nine-month programme.</p>

<h3>Strategy comes first</h3>

<p>Before any visual work: who you serve, what you do differently, what you want to be known for, and what you will deliberately not claim. This is the part that determines whether the rebrand achieves anything, and it is the part most often compressed because it produces no visible artefact.</p>

<p>A rebrand where the strategy phase was skipped produces a new logo attached to the same unclear positioning, which is why so many rebrands change how a company looks without changing how it performs.</p>

<h3>Verbal identity, not just visual</h3>

<p>The name, the descriptor that follows it, how you explain what you do in one sentence, the tone you write in, and the words you use for your own offering. Companies invest heavily in visual identity and then continue describing themselves in the same unclear language, which is the half customers actually encounter first.</p>

<h3>Visual identity</h3>

<p>Logo, typography, colour, imagery style, and the rules governing them. The logo receives most of the attention in the approval meeting and does the least work in practice — consistency of typography and colour across everything you produce carries far more recognition than the mark does.</p>

<h3>The application inventory</h3>

<p>Make the list before committing: website, social profiles, email signatures, templates, contracts, invoices, proposals, signage, vehicles, uniforms, packaging, app listings, directory entries, review profiles, advertising accounts. Every item is work, and the ones people forget — directory entries, old review profiles, third-party listings — are the ones that leave a company visibly half-rebranded a year later.</p>

<h3>Guidelines someone will actually follow</h3>

<p>A short, practical document beats a beautiful hundred-page manual nobody opens. It needs to answer the questions people actually have: which logo file, what spacing, which colours for what, what to do when the layout does not fit the rule.</p>

<h2>A framework for deciding</h2>

<p>Work through these in order. If you stall on the first, stop.</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>What specifically is the current brand costing us?</strong> Name it concretely — lost deals, wrong-fit enquiries, a market we cannot enter. If the answer is a feeling, the answer is no.</li>
  <li><strong>Would a refresh solve it?</strong> If the problem is that the identity looks dated, yes. Refresh is cheaper and preserves recognition.</li>
  <li><strong>Is the problem actually positioning?</strong> If prospects misunderstand what you do, changing the logo will not help. Change the message first — it is nearly free by comparison.</li>
  <li><strong>What do we lose?</strong> Recognition, search visibility, existing materials. Quantify roughly.</li>
  <li><strong>Can we sustain the transition?</strong> A rebrand needs months of consistent communication. Half-executed rebrands leave a company with two identities and neither working.</li>
  <li><strong>What will we measure?</strong> Decide before starting. Unmeasured rebrands are always declared successful.</li>
</ol>

<p>Answer question six in writing. Recognition, enquiry quality, and whether people describe you correctly are all measurable if you record a baseline first — and impossible to assess afterwards if you did not.</p>

<p>Question three catches the most cases. A great deal of rebranding effort is spent on visual identity when the actual problem is that the company has never clearly articulated what it does and for whom.</p>

<h2>Doing it without losing what you have</h2>

<p><strong>Tell existing customers first.</strong> They should hear it from you, before the announcement, with an explanation of what does and does not change for them.</p>

<p><strong>Overlap the identities.</strong> Run "X, formerly Y" for a period. It feels awkward internally and it is how people bridge the two in their memory.</p>

<p><strong>Keep the old domain forever, redirecting.</strong> Not for a year. Links and bookmarks persist much longer than anyone expects, and letting the domain lapse hands it to whoever wants it.</p>

<p><strong>Update everything in one coordinated push.</strong> A half-rebranded presence — new site, old social profiles, old email signatures — reads as disorganised rather than as transitional.</p>

<p><strong>Brief anyone customer-facing.</strong> They will be asked. A consistent answer matters more than a polished one.</p>

<p><strong>Write down the reasoning.</strong> Six months later, when someone asks why, an actual answer is worth having.</p>

<p><strong>Claim the new name everywhere at once.</strong> Social handles, directory listings, review profiles, app stores, and any industry register you appear in. Anything left under the old name becomes a stale duplicate that competes with you in search results and confuses anyone who finds it — and reclaiming a handle someone else has taken in the interim is often impossible.</p>

<p>Consistency afterwards is what makes it stick. That means the same positioning everywhere, sustained past the point where it feels repetitive internally — the same discipline that makes <a href="/blog/key-elements-of-an-effective-social-media-marketing-strategy">consistent positioning</a> work on any channel.</p>

<h2>Testing the new identity before you commit</h2>

<p>Rebrands are usually approved in a room full of people who have been looking at the options for weeks. That is the worst possible condition for judging something a customer will see for two seconds.</p>

<p>Three checks are worth running before signing off.</p>

<p><strong>Show it cold.</strong> Put the new name and identity in front of people who know nothing about the project — ideally customers rather than colleagues — and ask what kind of company they think it is. If the answers are consistently wrong, that is information you want now rather than after the signage is made.</p>

<p><strong>Check it where it will actually appear.</strong> A logo approved as a large image on a white slide behaves differently as a favicon, a social avatar, an embroidered polo shirt or a single-colour print. Review it at the smallest and most awkward sizes it will genuinely be used at.</p>

<p><strong>Search the name properly.</strong> Trademark databases, domain availability, social handles, and a plain search to see what already occupies that name — including in other languages and other markets. Discovering a conflict after launch is expensive and entirely avoidable.</p>

<h3>Do not test by committee vote</h3>

<p>Asking staff to vote between three options measures internal preference, not customer comprehension. Use testing to check whether the identity communicates what you intend, and let one accountable person make the decision. Design chosen by ballot reliably produces the least objectionable option rather than the most effective one.</p>

<h2>Timing</h2>

<p>Reasonable moments: alongside a genuine change in what the business does; at the start of a financial year when budgets and materials reset; ahead of entering a new market; or after a merger, once the combined entity is settled.</p>

<p>Poor moments: during a crisis, where it reads as deflection; immediately before your peak trading season; while the leadership team is still arguing about the direction; or when budget exists but a reason does not.</p>

<p>That last one is more common than it should be. An available budget is not a reason to spend it on identity.</p>

<p>One further consideration on timing: a rebrand consumes senior attention for months, not just money. If the leadership team is simultaneously managing a funding round, a systems migration or a period of rapid hiring, the rebrand will be under-supervised and the compromises will show. It is better postponed than half-run.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>When should a company rebrand?</h3>
<p>When the current identity actively misrepresents what the business is, or prevents it becoming what it intends to be — a name that constrains expansion, positioning that attracts the wrong customers, or a company that has genuinely changed. If you cannot name a specific cost the current brand imposes, a rebrand will not fix anything.</p>

<h3>What are the signs a business needs a rebrand?</h3>
<p>Prospects consistently arrive with the wrong expectation of what you do; the name limits geography or product range; you have merged and two identities persist; or the audience has shifted and the brand still addresses the previous one. Internal boredom with the logo is not a sign.</p>

<h3>What is the difference between a rebrand and a brand refresh?</h3>
<p>A refresh updates the visual identity while keeping the name, positioning and promise — low risk, recognition preserved. A full rebrand changes name and identity together, often the domain as well, and resets recognition. Repositioning sits between them: what you stand for changes while the visuals may not.</p>

<h3>Will rebranding hurt our SEO?</h3>
<p>Only if the domain changes, and then only if the migration is handled carelessly. A domain change is a full site migration: every URL must be mapped and redirected to its closest equivalent, and the old domain kept and redirecting indefinitely. Done properly it is survivable; done as an afterthought to the design work it is where rebrands cause lasting damage.</p>

<h3>How much does a rebrand cost?</h3>
<p>It varies by scope more than by anything else, and the design fee is usually the smaller part. The larger costs are replacing every material and system carrying the old identity, the communication effort through the transition, and the recognition you start rebuilding. Scope the second set before committing to the first.</p>

<h2>Before you commit</h2>

<p>Write one sentence naming what the current brand is costing you, and one sentence describing what would be true in a year if the rebrand worked. If either sentence is hard to write, the decision is not ready — and that is a useful outcome, because it is far cheaper to discover now.</p>

The visual side, including <a href="/blog/which-colors-should-i-use-for-my-website-and-why">a new palette</a>, is the last decision rather than the first.</p>
