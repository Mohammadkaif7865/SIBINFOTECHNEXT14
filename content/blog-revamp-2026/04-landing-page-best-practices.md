```html
<p>A landing page has one job: let someone who arrived with a specific expectation complete a specific action, with as little friction and doubt as possible. Almost every landing page problem traces back to violating one half of that sentence — either the expectation was not matched, or the action was made harder than it needed to be.</p>

<p>What follows is the order to work through when a page underperforms, and the structure to build from when starting fresh.</p>

<h2>What makes a landing page different</h2>

<p>A landing page is not a normal website page with a form on it. The distinction is that a landing page is built for one audience arriving from one source with one intention, which means you can remove everything that does not serve that intention.</p>

<p>A homepage must serve every visitor, so it offers many paths. A landing page serves one visitor type, so it offers one. That constraint is the entire source of its effectiveness. The moment a landing page starts accumulating secondary offers, navigation links and unrelated calls to action, it becomes a normal page and performs like one.</p>

<h2>Message match comes before everything</h2>

<p>The single most common cause of a poor-performing landing page is not the page. It is the gap between what the ad, email or link promised and what the page appears to be about.</p>

<p>Someone clicks an ad for "same-day AC repair in Andheri" and lands on a page headed "Comprehensive Home Services Solutions." The page may be well designed and fast. It has already failed, because the visitor spends their first two seconds establishing whether they are in the right place, and the answer looked like no.</p>

<h3>How to check message match in a minute</h3>

<p>Put the source and the page side by side. The ad headline, or the email subject, or the link text — and the page's H1. If the same nouns and the same specificity do not appear in both, that is your first fix, and it is usually the cheapest one available.</p>

<p>This is also why one landing page cannot serve five different campaigns well. If the traffic sources make different promises, the page can only match one of them. Separate pages, or a shared template with a swapped headline, will outperform a single generic page addressing everyone.</p>

<h2>What belongs above the fold</h2>

<p>The area visible on arrival has to answer three questions fast enough that the visitor does not leave while deciding: what is this, is it for me, and what do I do next.</p>

<p>In practice that means:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>A headline stating the specific outcome</strong>, not a slogan. "Get your AC repaired today in Andheri" beats "Comfort, delivered."</li>
  <li><strong>A subhead handling the obvious objection</strong> or adding the qualifying detail — price range, timeframe, who it is for.</li>
  <li><strong>One visible call to action.</strong> Not three.</li>
  <li><strong>A single credibility signal</strong> — a recognisable client, a certification, a specific number you can substantiate.</li>
</ul>

<p>Resist the pull to put everything above the fold. The goal is not to fit the whole argument onto the first screen; it is to earn the scroll. People scroll willingly when the top of the page convinced them the rest is relevant.</p>

<h2>A structure that holds up</h2>

<p>Most effective landing pages follow roughly this order, because it mirrors the sequence of questions a sceptical person asks.</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Section</th><th>The question it answers</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Headline and subhead</td><td>What is this, and is it for me?</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Primary call to action</td><td>What do you want me to do?</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Outcome, stated concretely</td><td>What changes for me if I do it?</td></tr>
    <tr><td>How it works</td><td>What actually happens — am I committing to something opaque?</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Proof</td><td>Why should I believe you?</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Objection handling</td><td>What about the thing I am worried about?</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Repeated call to action</td><td>Fine — where do I do it?</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>Repeat the call to action at least twice on any page longer than two screens. People decide at different points, and making a convinced visitor scroll back up to find the button costs conversions for no reason.</p>

<h3>Write the outcome, not the feature list</h3>

<p>Feature lists describe what you built. Outcomes describe what changes for the reader. "Automated monthly reporting" is a feature. "You stop spending the first Monday of every month building the same report" is the outcome. Most pages are heavy on the first and light on the second, and the second is what persuades.</p>

<h2>Reduce friction, deliberately</h2>

<p>Friction is anything between intention and completion. Some of it is necessary. Most is not, and it accumulates by default rather than by decision.</p>

<h3>Cut form fields to what you will actually use</h3>

<p>Every field costs completions. The test for each one is not "would this be useful to know" — it is "will someone act on this within the next week?" Job title, company size and "how did you hear about us" usually fail that test and are collected out of habit.</p>

<p>You can always ask for more later, once there is a relationship. Asking for it upfront trades a conversion for data you will not use.</p>

<h3>Remove navigation from dedicated campaign pages</h3>

<p>A full site header on a paid landing page is an invitation to leave. For pages receiving paid traffic with a single objective, strip the navigation to a logo and, where required, legal links. This is one of the few changes that reliably helps and costs almost nothing to try.</p>

<p>Note the exception: pages that also need to rank organically, or that serve visitors still researching, benefit from navigation. Do not apply this to every page reflexively.</p>

<h3>Make the button say what happens</h3>

<p>"Submit" describes the mechanism. "Get my free quote" describes the result. Labels that state the outcome consistently outperform labels that describe the form action, because they reduce uncertainty about what comes next.</p>

<p>Set expectations immediately below the button: what happens now, how quickly, and whether it commits them to anything. "We'll call within one working day. No obligation." removes more hesitation than another paragraph of copy.</p>

<h3>Handle errors without punishing people</h3>

<p>Validate inline rather than on submit. Preserve what was already entered when something fails. Say what is wrong and how to fix it. A form that clears itself after an error loses most of the people it happens to, and it is a bug that survives because nobody tests the failure path.</p>

<h2>Proof that persuades</h2>

<p>Not all social proof works. Generic praise is close to worthless because it is indistinguishable from invented praise.</p>

<p>What carries weight is specificity: a named person at a named organisation describing a concrete situation, a result you can substantiate, a recognisable logo, a relevant certification, a case with enough detail to be checkable.</p>

<p>Place proof next to the claim it supports rather than in a testimonial block at the bottom. A concern about response time is best answered by proof about response time, positioned where the concern arises.</p>

<p>One caution worth taking seriously: never publish a result, review or statistic you cannot substantiate if challenged. Beyond the ethics, unverifiable claims are the sort of thing that gets removed later at cost, and specific-sounding numbers with no source behind them do not persuade sceptical readers anyway.</p>

<h2>The page has to work on a phone</h2>

<p>Most landing page traffic arrives on mobile, and most landing pages are designed on a desktop monitor. The gap shows up as forms that are painful to complete, buttons below an on-screen keyboard, and hero sections that push the call to action off the visible area entirely.</p>

<p>Check specifically: does the form use the right keyboard for each field type, is the primary button reachable one-handed, does the page hold together at 360px wide, and does the layout stay still while loading. A layout shift that moves the button as someone taps it is among the most costly bugs a landing page can have, and it is invisible on a fast desktop connection.</p>

<p>The broader mechanics of making a page <a href="/blog/does-your-website-need-a-mobile-makeover">work properly on a phone</a> apply directly here, and landing pages are where the cost of getting it wrong is most immediate.</p>

<h2>Speed is part of the offer</h2>

<p>A landing page that takes four seconds to become useful has lost a share of its traffic before the copy is read. This matters more for paid traffic, where every lost visitor was purchased.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://web.dev/articles/vitals" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Core Web Vitals thresholds</a> are a reasonable target: main content visible within 2.5 seconds, interaction responding within 200 milliseconds, and layout shift under 0.1. On landing pages the most common causes of failure are an oversized hero image, a font that reflows text when it loads, and third-party scripts — chat widgets, session recorders, tag managers — that block the main thread.</p>

<p>Landing pages are also where the tracking script count quietly grows. Audit what is loading; each tag is paid for on every visit.</p>

<h2>The page after the page</h2>

<p>The thank-you page is the most neglected asset in most funnels. It is shown at the exact moment someone has just demonstrated trust, and it is usually a blank confirmation.</p>

<p>Use it. Restate what happens next and when, so nobody wonders whether the form worked. Give them something to do in the meantime — a relevant case, a scheduling link, a genuinely useful resource. Where appropriate, this is the natural place to invite the next step, because a person who just converted is more receptive than they will be at any other point.</p>

<p>It is also where conversion tracking usually fires, which makes it worth confirming that the page is reachable only after a real submission. Thank-you pages that are publicly linked or indexable inflate your conversion data with visits that never converted.</p>

<h2>Test properly or not at all</h2>

<p>A/B testing is worth doing and easy to do badly. Two rules prevent most of the damage.</p>

<p><strong>Change one thing at a time.</strong> If you change the headline, the image and the form together and conversions rise, you have learned that the new page is better, but nothing about why — so you cannot apply it anywhere else.</p>

<p><strong>Decide the sample size and duration before starting.</strong> Stopping a test the moment it looks favourable is how teams accumulate confident conclusions that do not replicate. Run for full weeks to cover day-of-week variation, and do not read results daily hoping for significance.</p>

<p>If your traffic volume is too low for statistical significance in a reasonable timeframe — which is the honest situation for many businesses — do not run A/B tests. Make changes based on qualitative evidence instead: session recordings, direct feedback, and watching real people attempt the task. That evidence is often more actionable than an underpowered test.</p>

<h2>Landing page optimization checklist</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Headline uses the same specific language as the traffic source</li>
  <li>The offer is stated as an outcome, not a feature</li>
  <li>One primary call to action, repeated on longer pages</li>
  <li>Button label describes the result, not the mechanism</li>
  <li>Expectation set below the button — what happens next, and how fast</li>
  <li>Form asks only for what will be acted on</li>
  <li>Navigation removed on dedicated paid campaign pages</li>
  <li>Proof is specific, attributed and placed beside the relevant claim</li>
  <li>No claim published that cannot be substantiated</li>
  <li>Renders and converts correctly at 360px width</li>
  <li>Form fields trigger the correct mobile keyboard</li>
  <li>Core Web Vitals passing on mobile</li>
  <li>Inline validation, with entered data preserved on error</li>
  <li>Sufficient contrast on text and buttons</li>
  <li>Analytics recording the conversion, verified by an actual test submission</li>
</ul>

<p>That last item is worth checking first on any existing page. Broken conversion tracking is more common than it should be, and it makes every other optimisation unmeasurable.</p>

<h2>Mistakes that recur</h2>

<p><strong>One page for every campaign.</strong> A single generic page cannot match five different promises. It matches none of them well.</p>

<p><strong>Designing for the decision-maker who approves it.</strong> Internal stakeholders ask for more information, more options and more logos. Visitors want fewer.</p>

<p><strong>Burying the call to action under a brand story.</strong> Someone arriving from a specific ad has already decided they are interested. Making them read a company history first tests that interest unnecessarily.</p>

<p><strong>Treating contrast as a style choice.</strong> Low-contrast text and pale buttons look refined on a design monitor and disappear on a phone in daylight. The same reasoning covered in <a href="/blog/which-colors-should-i-use-for-my-website-and-why">contrast and colour decisions</a> applies with more force here, because there is a transaction at stake.</p>

<p><strong>Optimising the page while ignoring the traffic.</strong> If the audience is wrong, no page fixes it. Poor-quality traffic converts poorly on excellent pages.</p>

<h2>When a landing page is the wrong tool</h2>

<p>For complex, high-value purchases with long consideration cycles, a single page asking for a decision is often the wrong instrument. What works better is a lower-commitment step — a genuinely useful resource, a diagnostic, a conversation — followed by nurture. The mechanics of that longer path are covered in <a href="/blog/how-to-convert-website-visitors-into-leads">converting visitors into leads</a>.</p>

<p>Landing pages also underperform when the underlying offer is weak. A page can present an offer clearly; it cannot make an unattractive offer attractive. If a page has been through several honest iterations without moving, the constraint is probably the offer or the audience, not the layout.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>What are the most important landing page best practices?</h3>
<p>Match the page's headline to the promise made by the ad or email that sent the visitor; state the outcome rather than the feature set; use one primary call to action; ask only for form fields you will act on; place specific, attributed proof beside the claims it supports; and make sure it loads fast and works on a phone. Message match first — it is the cheapest fix and the most common failure.</p>

<h3>How long should a landing page be?</h3>
<p>Long enough to answer the questions a sceptical visitor has, and no longer. Low-commitment offers need little. High-commitment or high-price offers need more, because more objections exist. Length is a consequence of the offer, not a target to hit.</p>

<h3>Should a landing page have navigation?</h3>
<p>Remove it on dedicated pages receiving paid traffic with a single objective — every navigation link is an exit. Keep it on pages that also need to rank organically or that serve visitors still researching, where restricting movement frustrates people who are not ready to convert.</p>

<h3>What is landing page conversion optimization?</h3>
<p>The practice of systematically removing the reasons people do not complete the intended action — mismatch with the traffic source, unclear offer, unnecessary friction, unanswered objections, technical faults. It is diagnostic work rather than redesign work: the aim is to find the specific blockage, not to make the page look better.</p>

<h3>How many form fields should a landing page have?</h3>
<p>As few as the next step genuinely requires. For most first contacts that is a name and one contact method. Each additional field costs completions, and information you collect but never act on is a pure loss. You can ask for more once a relationship exists.</p>

<h2>Where to start</h2>

<p>Take your worst-performing page and do three things in this order. Confirm the conversion is actually being tracked. Put the traffic source and the page headline side by side and fix the mismatch. Then delete every form field nobody acts on. Those three, in that order, resolve more underperformance than any redesign.</p>

<p>If the pages are sound and the constraint is having someone run the diagnosis and testing programme continuously, that is what our <a href="/conversion-rate-optimization">conversion rate optimisation</a> work covers — research, testing and implementation as an ongoing cycle rather than a one-off audit.</p>
```
