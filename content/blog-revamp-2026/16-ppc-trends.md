```html
<p>The significant change in paid search is that the platforms now build and target campaigns better than most advertisers can by hand. What remains under your control is the input: your data, your creative, your offer, and your judgement about what to measure. That reversal explains almost every genuine trend below.</p>

<p>It also means a good deal of current PPC advice is describing skills the platforms have absorbed. Here is what has actually changed.</p>

<h2>Start with a correction: cookies are not going away</h2>

<p>A great deal of PPC content still advises preparing for a "cookieless future" and a hard deadline for third-party cookie deprecation in Chrome. <strong>That deadline no longer exists.</strong></p>

<p>Google <a href="https://privacysandbox.com/news/privacy-sandbox-next-steps/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">announced it is maintaining its approach of offering users choice</a> over third-party cookies in Chrome, rather than deprecating them, and will not introduce a standalone prompt. Privacy Sandbox continues, with a different role than originally planned.</p>

<p>This matters for two reasons. Advice premised on an imminent cutoff is out of date, and you should treat any article still repeating it as unmaintained. But the underlying direction has not reversed — signal loss is real and continuing, driven by browser tracking protections, operating-system privacy controls, ad blockers and regulation. The deadline vanished; the trend did not.</p>

<p>The practical conclusion is unchanged: build first-party data, implement server-side conversion measurement, and stop depending on third-party tracking. Do it because it works, not because of a date that was cancelled.</p>

<h2>AI now builds the campaign</h2>

<p>The largest genuine shift. Campaign construction, targeting and bidding have moved substantially from the advertiser to the platform.</p>

<h3>AI Max for Search</h3>

<p>Google's <a href="https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/15910366" target="_blank" rel="noopener">AI Max for Search campaigns</a> bundles several automation features into search campaigns: search term matching that expands beyond your keywords using broad match and keywordless technology, text customisation that generates headlines and descriptions from your landing page and existing assets, and final URL expansion that selects the most relevant destination for each query.</p>

<p>Google reports an average of 7% more conversions or conversion value at similar CPA or ROAS when the full suite is used compared with search term matching alone. That is the platform's own published figure and should be read as such — your result depends on your account, and the honest way to establish it is to test rather than to assume.</p>

<p>The direction of travel is clear: Dynamic Search Ads are being upgraded to AI Max, and the feature set is extending to Shopping and travel formats.</p>

<h3>Performance Max</h3>

<p><a href="https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/10724817" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Performance Max</a> runs a single campaign across Search, Display, YouTube, Discover, Gmail and Maps, with Google allocating budget and placement. Early versions were criticised for opacity; controls have since improved — campaign-level negative keywords are now available to all advertisers, and high-value new customer mode lets you signal which customers you want more of via Customer Match.</p>

<h3>What is left for the advertiser</h3>

<p>This is the useful question, and the answer is more than "nothing".</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>The platform now handles</th><th>You still control</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Bid setting per auction</td><td>What counts as a conversion, and what it is worth</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Query matching and expansion</td><td>The data you feed it</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Placement and format allocation</td><td>Creative and offer</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Asset combination and testing</td><td>Budget allocation across campaigns</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Audience discovery</td><td>Exclusions, brand safety and negatives</td></tr>
    <tr><td></td><td>Whether the reported result is real</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>The last row is where experienced advertisers now add most of their value. Automation optimises toward the goal it is given. If the goal is badly defined — counting low-quality leads as conversions, valuing all conversions equally — it will optimise efficiently toward the wrong outcome, and the reporting will look healthy while the business result is not.</p>

<h2>First-party data is the durable advantage</h2>

<p>When targeting is automated, the differentiator is what you give the system to work with.</p>

<p>Customer lists, site behaviour, CRM data on which leads actually closed, and offline conversion imports are the inputs that let automation optimise toward customers rather than toward conversions. Two advertisers running identical campaign types with the same budget will get materially different results if one is feeding back which leads became revenue and the other is not.</p>

<h3>What this means practically</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Import offline conversions.</strong> For any business where the sale completes off-site, this is the single highest-value integration available. It teaches the platform which clicks became money.</li>
  <li><strong>Use enhanced or server-side conversion measurement</strong> alongside browser tags. Browser-based tracking loses a meaningful share of events, and the optimisation degrades with the data.</li>
  <li><strong>Segment your seed audiences.</strong> A list of your highest-value customers produces better similar audiences than a list of everyone who ever bought.</li>
  <li><strong>Build the list deliberately.</strong> This is a strategic reason to invest in <a href="/blog/how-email-marketing-impacts-lead-generation-on-digital-platform">building a first-party list</a> rather than only buying traffic.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Creative has become the main lever</h2>

<p>If the platform selects placements and audiences, the asset is what determines whether the impression works.</p>

<p>Automated campaign types consume large volumes of assets — headlines, descriptions, images, video — and combine them. Feeding them a thin set constrains what the system can do. Feeding them genuinely different concepts, rather than minor variations, gives it something to optimise between.</p>

<p>Two practical points. Test distinct angles rather than cosmetic changes: problem-led against outcome-led, price-led against quality-led. And refresh continuously, because performance decays as an audience sees the same asset repeatedly — rising cost per result with unchanged targeting is usually fatigue.</p>

<p>Generative tools now assist with asset production, which lowers the cost of volume but not the cost of judgement. A larger quantity of undifferentiated assets does not help; the constraint is having something worth saying.</p>

<h2>Measurement is the hardest part</h2>

<p>Attribution has become genuinely harder, and confident reporting is often the least trustworthy kind.</p>

<h3>Modelled conversions are not observed conversions</h3>

<p>Where tracking is unavailable, platforms estimate conversions using modelling. This is reasonable and it is not the same as counting. Reports blend observed and modelled data, usually without distinguishing them, so precision in the interface does not imply precision in reality.</p>

<h3>Platforms grade their own work</h3>

<p>Each platform attributes using its own window and its own model, and each has an interest in the answer. Sum conversions across two ad platforms and your analytics and you will typically exceed your actual order count. Use platform data for optimisation inside the platform; use your own analytics and CRM to judge business impact; never add them together.</p>

<h3>Incrementality is the question that matters</h3>

<p>Attribution asks which touchpoint preceded a conversion. Incrementality asks whether the conversion would have happened anyway — a materially different and more useful question, particularly for branded search and retargeting, where a substantial share of conversions would often have occurred without the ad.</p>

<p>The rigorous method is a holdout: suspend spend for a segment or region while maintaining another, and observe what genuinely changes. It costs real money and it is the only way to distinguish influence from coincidence. Few advertisers run these, and the ones that do usually find at least one campaign producing less than its reported figures suggest.</p>

<h2>Working with automation rather than against it</h2>

<p>The most common complaint about automated campaign types is loss of control. Some of that is real; much of it is a habit of intervening in ways that now make performance worse.</p>

<h3>Give it time to learn</h3>

<p>Automated bidding needs a period of data accumulation before performance settles. Editing budgets, bids or targeting during that period restarts it. Accounts that are adjusted daily frequently perform worse than accounts left alone, because they never leave the noisy phase.</p>

<p>Set a review cadence — fortnightly is usually reasonable — and resist reacting to individual days.</p>

<h3>Consolidate rather than fragment</h3>

<p>The instinct to split campaigns finely was correct when humans set bids. It is counterproductive now: each campaign needs sufficient conversion volume to optimise, and dividing a modest budget across many campaigns starves all of them. Fewer campaigns with more data generally outperform many precise ones.</p>

<h3>Steer with inputs, not overrides</h3>

<p>Where you want a different outcome, change what the system optimises toward rather than fighting its decisions. Adjust conversion values, supply better audience signals, exclude what you do not want, and improve the assets. These are the levers that still work.</p>

<h3>Keep watching what it matches</h3>

<p>Broader matching means your ads appear against queries you did not choose. Review search terms regularly and add negatives. This is one of the few genuinely manual tasks that has become more important rather than less, and it is routinely neglected precisely because the campaign type is described as automated.</p>

<h2>Costs, and what to do about them</h2>

<p>Auction costs generally trend upward as more advertisers compete for the same inventory. That is structural rather than a passing condition, and the response is not to bid harder.</p>

<p>What actually helps:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Improve conversion rate rather than reducing cost per click.</strong> A page converting at twice the rate halves your cost per acquisition without touching the auction — which is why <a href="/blog/ways-to-make-your-website-s-landing-page-a-conversion-machine">the page the click lands on</a> is a paid search concern, not a separate project.</li>
  <li><strong>Raise the value of a customer.</strong> Better retention and higher order value let you afford more per acquisition than competitors bidding on the same terms.</li>
  <li><strong>Compete where others are not.</strong> Specific, lower-volume queries carry clearer intent and cost less than head terms.</li>
  <li><strong>Feed the algorithm value, not just conversions.</strong> Reporting conversion value rather than conversion counts lets bidding pursue profitable customers instead of cheap ones.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Paid search is no longer only search</h2>

<p>Automated campaign types span search, video, display and discovery in one unit, which makes "search advertising" and "display advertising" less separable than they were. Meanwhile the surfaces people search on continue to broaden — retail platforms, video, social and AI assistants all take queries that once went only to a search engine.</p>

<p>The practical implication is not to be everywhere. It is to know where your customers actually search, which is a research question rather than a channel-adoption question. The paid social side is a distinct discipline covered in <a href="/blog/social-media-advertising-tips-and-tricks">paid social</a>.</p>

<h2>Compliance is now part of running ads</h2>

<p>An area that used to be somebody else's problem and now affects delivery and measurement directly.</p>

<p><strong>Consent affects your data.</strong> In regions requiring consent for advertising cookies, whether and how you collect it determines what your conversion tracking observes. Implemented poorly, you lose measurement quality and the optimisation degrades with it. Implemented properly, modelling fills more of the gap.</p>

<p><strong>Restricted categories carry extra rules.</strong> Finance, health, employment, housing and credit face additional limits on claims and on how audiences may be targeted. Read the policies for your category before producing creative rather than after a disapproval.</p>

<p><strong>Repeated violations escalate.</strong> Individual disapprovals are routine and recoverable. A pattern can restrict an account. Where a rejection looks wrong, appeal rather than resubmitting slightly edited versions, which tends to compound the problem.</p>

<p>None of this is difficult once handled deliberately. It becomes expensive when discovered mid-campaign.</p>

<h2>What has not changed</h2>

<p>Useful, because it is where most accounts still lose money.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Conversion tracking must be verified.</strong> Campaigns optimising on broken tracking optimise toward nothing. Complete a real conversion and confirm it registers.</li>
  <li><strong>The destination page decides the outcome.</strong> Paid traffic is purchased; a poor page wastes it at a known cost.</li>
  <li><strong>Negatives still matter.</strong> Automation matches more broadly, which makes exclusions more important, not less.</li>
  <li><strong>Structure follows the business.</strong> Campaigns should map to what you sell and its margins, not to an account template.</li>
  <li><strong>A weak offer cannot be optimised into a strong one.</strong> If an account has been through several honest iterations without improving, the constraint is usually the offer or the audience rather than the campaign settings.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What to actually do this quarter</h2>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Verify conversion tracking</strong> by completing a real conversion yourself.</li>
  <li><strong>Check what you count as a conversion.</strong> If low-quality leads count equally, automation is optimising toward them.</li>
  <li><strong>Import offline conversions</strong> if your sale completes off-site. Highest-value change available to most accounts.</li>
  <li><strong>Implement server-side or enhanced conversion measurement</strong> alongside browser tags.</li>
  <li><strong>Refresh your creative</strong>, testing distinct angles rather than variations.</li>
  <li><strong>Run one holdout test</strong> on a campaign you assume is working. Expect to be surprised.</li>
  <li><strong>Audit negatives and exclusions</strong>, which matter more under broad matching.</li>
  <li><strong>Fix your highest-spend landing page</strong> before increasing budget.</li>
</ol>

<p>None of these are exotic. Steps one and two are where most of the wasted spend in most accounts originates, and both can be completed in an afternoon by someone who already has access.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>What are the latest PPC trends?</h3>
<p>Campaign construction, targeting and bidding have moved substantially to platform automation, which shifts the advertiser's job to data quality, creative and defining the right goal. First-party data has become the main differentiator, creative the main lever, and incrementality testing the only reliable way to know what advertising is actually producing.</p>

<h3>Are third-party cookies going away?</h3>
<p>No. Google reversed its plan to deprecate third-party cookies in Chrome and is maintaining user choice instead, with no standalone prompt. Advice still describing an imminent cookieless deadline is out of date. Signal loss from browser protections, OS privacy controls and regulation continues regardless, so building first-party data remains the right response.</p>

<h3>Should I use automated campaign types like Performance Max?</h3>
<p>Usually yes, provided your conversion tracking is accurate and your conversion definition is sound. Automation optimises efficiently toward whatever goal it is given, so it amplifies both good and bad goal definitions. Controls have improved — campaign-level negative keywords and new customer signals are now available. Test against your existing structure rather than switching wholesale.</p>

<h3>Is manual bidding still worth using?</h3>
<p>Rarely. Automated bidding evaluates signals per auction that no human can act on, and generally outperforms manual on the same budget. Reserve manual intervention for specific reasons — a hard cost ceiling the business cannot exceed, or a placement consistently producing poor-quality traffic.</p>

<h3>Why do my ad platform and analytics report different numbers?</h3>
<p>They answer different questions with different rules and different attribution windows, and platform figures blend observed with modelled conversions. Expect disagreement, treat the two as bounds rather than picking the flattering one, and use a holdout test when you need to know what is genuinely incremental.</p>

<h2>The through-line</h2>

<p>Every trend above points the same way: the platforms have taken the mechanical work, and what remains is judgement — what you measure, what you feed the system, what you say, and whether you believe the reported result.</p>

<p>Accounts that struggle now are rarely struggling on bid management. They are struggling because the conversion definition is wrong, the data going in is thin, or nobody has checked whether the numbers coming out describe anything real.</p>

<p>That is a more uncomfortable diagnosis than a tactical one, because it cannot be fixed by changing a setting. It is also why the accounts that improve most are usually the ones where somebody was willing to question a number that everybody had been reporting for a year.</p>
```
